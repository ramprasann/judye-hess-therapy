“
All companionship can consist only in the strengthening of two neighboring solitudes, whereas everything that one is wont to call giving oneself is by nature harmful to companionship: for when a person abandons himself, he is no longer anything, and when two people both give themselves up in order to come close to each other, there is no longer any ground beneath them, and their living together becomes a breathless falling…
— Rainer Maria Rilke, Letters to a Young Poet Clinical reflection by Judye Hess, Ph.D.

The Paradox of Giving Oneself Away

In popular romantic folklore, enduring love is constantly framed through the lens of total fusion. We are inundated with narratives suggesting that true devotion requires dissolving one’s boundaries, accommodating a partner’s emotional turbulence at any personal expense, and sacrificing our sovereign voice on the altar of relational harmony.

Yet in over four decades of clinical practice sitting with couples, I have observed that this ideal of unmeasured self-abnegation is precisely what suffocates romance. When an individual abandons their core self—their discrete opinions, creative pursuits, private silences, and moral limits—they inadvertently eliminate the very partner the other person fell in love with. Fusion does not yield closeness; it yields anxiety, enmeshment, and a quiet, toxic reservoir of submerged resentment.

When two individuals sacrifice their footing to satisfy an anxious demand for closeness, there is indeed "no longer any ground beneath them." The partnership ceases to be an encounter between two autonomous adults and becomes instead a breathless, precarious free-fall where every minor difference is experienced as an existential threat.

Two Neighboring Solitudes

Rilke’s brilliant phrasing—two neighboring solitudes—captures what clinical theory terms differentiation. Differentiation is the capacity to remain anchored in oneself while staying emotionally connected to an important other. It means having an intact emotional skin: able to be influenced and warmed by your partner, but not dissolved or hijacked by their shifting moods.

During my years training doctoral candidates and marital therapists at the California Institute of Integral Studies (CIIS), we regularly examined how genuine intimacy is impossible without separation. If there is no real separateness, there is no bridge to cross; there is merely a merged terrain where neither partner feels safe enough to disagree, to withdraw into productive contemplation, or to express distinct desires.

When each partner holds a respected inner sanctuary, their time together becomes an invitation rather than an obligation. The paradox is that having room to retreat, to contemplate, and to inhabit one's own center makes returning to one another profoundly restorative.

Clinical Practice in the East Bay & Telehealth

In my Berkeley office and in video consultations across California, this dynamic frequently surfaces during moments of acute relational exhaustion. One partner complains of feeling chronically managed or pursued; the other laments emotional abandonment.

Through Core Focused Family Therapy, we trace the historical roots of these reflexes. Often, the urge to merge stems from childhood environments where love had to be earned by managing a parent’s volatile feelings. When couples learn to unhook from this archaic contract, a miraculous calm enters the room. They realize that a partner taking an afternoon solo hike, pursuing an eccentric passion, or holding a dissenting opinion is not a rejection—it is the very breath that keeps the fire of attraction alive.

Questions to Consider in Your Relationship

01. Autonomy

Where in your daily routine do you preserve private time solely for your own thoughts, without checking in or seeking validation?

02. Boundaries

Can you witness your partner feeling anxious, disappointed, or sad without urgently leaping in to fix or absorb their emotional climate?

03. Merging

What dormant interest, circle of friendship, or intellectual passion might you reactivate to nourish your own unique center?
